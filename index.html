02 — Demo · Speaker Reference

Let's build something
with AI

For the next few minutes, imagine I'm as you client. I want to build a very simple banking application.

I want a login page, a dashboard, an account with a balance, and to list a beneficiary details, and a way to transfer money.

Normally, Developer will start thinking about the application structure and create the files, write the code, integrate the components together, run it, debug it and gradually turn the idea into an application.

But today, It is different. We simply describe our idea to AI.

The screen looks professional. The application runs abosolutely fine. AI developed it very quickly. But one important business rule is missing.

This is exactly the point I want to make. Fast does not automatically mean accurate.

AI can generate incredibly quickly, but that doesn't mean its answer is correct. Im not saying AI is wrong. Maybe our instruction wasn't clear enough. “We all know AI can sometimes hallucinate. Maybe we forgot to mention an edge case. So don't immediately think, “AI said it, so it must be right.”

When AI generates code, ask yourself: “Do I understand what this code is doing?” When it gives you an analysis, ask: “Does this numbers actually make sense?” And when it gives you an idea, ask: “Can I make this idea better?”

The lesson is: use AI for speed, but bring your own understanding.

We need to know what we asked for. We need to observe the result. We need to question it. We need to recognise what is missing. And then we can give AI a better instruction and improve it.

Idea→Generate→Observe→Question→Refine

One key takeway or what I want all of you to understand is : when AI gives you an answer, don't stop at “As I got an answer.” Go one step further and ask: “Is that answer accurate? Is it what I expected?”

AI can help us move incredibly fast. But we still have to decide where we're going.

03 — Real World Engineering · Speaker Reference

What happens
behind one click?

Next 10 15 minutes I would like to touch base on the real world engineering or how systems connects behind the screen on our one click: We learn about APIs, databases, cloud platforms, security, and data analystics. We often learn each technology separately. Now let's see how they come together in one experience.

Let us take an example of something that many of us have used: a ride booking app like uber, ola. Imagine there is a student named Diya. She walks out of the venue, opens Uber, enters her destination, sees a price and presses Book. That's it. From her point of view, she opened an app, entered a destination and pressed a button.

But let's freeze this moment. The second she presses Book, something much more interesting starts happening behind the screen.

First, the app needs to know where she is. Her phone provides location information through GPS. Then the application needs to find drivers nearby. There could be hundreds of drivers moving around the city. Does it simply choose the one who is physically closest? Not necessarily.

One driver may be only 500 metres away but travelling in the opposite direction. Another may be two kilometres away but able to reach her faster. There may be traffic, and a driver may still be finishing another trip. What looked like a simple Book button has become a decision making problem. The system needs to consider location, availability, direction, routes and traffic.

Somewhere behind that screen, the mobile app talks to APIs. APIs connect to backend services. Services work with databases. Location data keeps changing; maps and traffic information may be involved. cloud infrastructure keeps services running, security protects data

📱 App→📍 GPS→API→Services→🚗 Driver→Maps→Payment

Eventually, one driver receives a notification: Diya wants a ride. The driver accepts. Now Diya can see the vehicle number and an estimated arrival time: three minutes away. A moment later, she checks again and it still says three minutes. We've all watched that little car move around the map! Behind it, location updates, ETA calculations, route information and notifications keep flowing.

Then the journey starts. Traffic changes, the fare is calculated, the journey finishes, payment is processed, the driver gets paid, Diya receives a receipt, and the app asks her to rate the driver. All of that began with one action: Book a ride.

Now imagine that one service fails when Diya presses Book and nothing happens. She isn't going to say, “There is increased latency between two microservices.” She'll say, “Uber isn't working. Let's move to another app.”

Customers don't experience our architecture.
They experience the product.

What I want you to notice. We learn Java, Python, APIs, databases, cloud, AI, DevOps and cybersecurity as separate subjects. In the real world, technology doesn't work in separate boxes. These parts come together to create one experience.

From now on, stay curious about understanding what happens behind the screen. When you order food, what happens after you tap Order? When you make a payment, where does the request go? When Netflix recommends something, what happened behind that recommendation? When an OTP arrives two seconds after you request it, what happened in those two seconds?

You don't need every answer today. Start asking the questions. That curiosity takes you from learning individual technologies to thinking like an engineer.

04 — Road Map · Speaker Reference

Okay... what exactly
should I learn?

So what should I actually learn? Technology can feel overwhelming today. Every few months another tool appears on LinkedIn, almost telling us, “Learn me immediately!” Please don't panic. You don't have to learn everything.

If I were starting my technology career today, I would build my knowledge in layers.

Layer 1 — Learn to Think

The first layer isn't AI or cloud, or even a particular programming language. Learn to think through a problem. Learn programming fundamentals: conditions, loops, functions, objects and data. Learn basic data structures and algorithms. Practise taking one big problem and breaking it into smaller ones.

Use AI — absolutely use it. But when it gives you code, ask, “Why does this work?” Change something. Break something. Ask it to explain the part that you don't understand. That's how the answer becomes your understanding.

Layer 2 — Learn How Things Connect

Once you understand the fundamentals, look beyond one piece of code. Remember our ride booking example? The mobile screen was only one small part of the system. What is an API? What happens between the frontend and backend? Where does the data go? Why do we need a database? How does one service communicate with another? What happens when that communication fails?

You don't need to become an expert in everything. Start following the journey of the data. Working with large enterprise systems has taught me this repeatedlys.

Layer 3 — Learn Quality

This one is close to my heart because I work in quality engineering. I'm not saying everyone must become a tester. I'm saying that whatever you build, learn to question it. Ask both “Does it work?” and “How can it fail?”

Imagine you build a payment app. What if someone enters a negative amount? What if they press Pay twice? What if the network disappears halfway through? What if ten users suddenly become ten thousand? Get into the habit of asking, “Can I trust this?”

Layer 4 — Learn How Software Reaches the Real World

You write code today. How does it get from your laptop to thousands or millions of people? Start with the concepts behind Git, automated builds and tests, CI/CD, cloud and deployment.

Ask what happens after “I push my code.” How is it built? How is it tested automatically? How is it deployed? Where does it run? Once you understand that journey, CI/CD stops being just another abbreviation and it will starts to make sense.

put AI Across All of It

Use it while learning, coding, testing, debugging and exploring. Let it sit on top of your knowledge and amplify what you can do. But You are still responsible for understanding and validating the result.

Tools will change. Models will change. If your fundamentals are strong, AI becomes an amplifier of your ability.

And one final, important layer: communication. You might write brilliant code or discover something nobody else has noticed. Eventually you need to explain it to a teammate, a manager, a customer or an interviewer.

Understanding technology is powerful. Being able to make someone else understand it is another level of skill. Practise explaining what you built, why you built it, what went wrong and what you learned.

You don't have to master every new tool that appears in the market. Build these layers, stay curious and keep learning. That's a roadmap you can carry with you even when the tools change.

05 — Conclusion · Speaker Reference

Four things you can
start doing today

Before I close, I want to leave you with four things you can start doing today.

First, make a learning plan. Write down the technologies you want to learn. Pick one at a time, and spend one hour every day learning it. Treat that hour as an appointment with your future.

Second, build what you learn. Don’t stop after watching a tutorial. Create a small app or a simple project. When you build something yourself, your confidence grows.

Third, don’t be afraid of your first attempt. It may not work the way you expected—and that’s okay. It’s a chance to find out what needs to change and try again.

Fourth, stay consistent. Keep learning and building even after you get a job. That habit will help you grow throughout your career.

Technology will keep changing. But if you’re ready to think, learn, try, and adapt, you can build your own future.

With that thought, I’ll close my speech. Thank you once again for listening so patiently.
