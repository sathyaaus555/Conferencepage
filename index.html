PERFORM THE TRANSFER

And look at that — the application allows the transfer.

Now we have a problem. The screen looks professional. The application runs. AI produced it very quickly. But one important business rule is missing.

This is exactly the point I want to make. Fast does not automatically mean accurate.

AI can generate incredibly quickly, but it doesn't mean it is accurate. Maybe our instruction wasn't clear enough. Maybe it made an assumption. Maybe we forgot to mention an edge case.

The lesson is: use AI for speed, but bring your own understanding.

We need to know what we asked for. We need to observe the result. We need to question it. We need to recognise what is missing. And then we can give AI a better instruction and improve it.

Idea→Generate→Observe→Question→Refine

So when AI gives you an answer, don't stop at “I got an answer.” Go one step further and ask: “Is this actually the right answer?”

AI can help us move incredibly fast. But we still have to decide where we're going.

03 — Real World Engineering · Speaker Reference

What happens
behind one click?

Now let's move away from our small demo and look at something we use in real life.

Imagine a student named Diya leaves this conference. She opens a ride app, enters her destination and taps Book.

From Diya's point of view, that is almost the whole story. Open the app. Enter the destination. Book the ride. A driver appears.

But as engineers, we should be curious about what happened during those few seconds.

First, the app needs to know where Diya is. Her phone provides location information using GPS.

Then the application needs to find drivers around that location. But it is not simply a matter of picking the driver who looks closest on the map.

One driver may be physically close but travelling in the opposite direction. Another may already have a passenger. Another may be stuck in traffic. So the system has to consider location, availability, direction, distance, traffic and other information before deciding which driver to match.

That simple tap on the phone can trigger calls through APIs into backend services. Services process information. Databases may be read. Maps and traffic systems may be involved. Events and notifications move between different parts of the platform.

📱 App→📍 GPS→API→Services→🚗 Driver→Maps→Payment

Once a driver accepts, Diya sees the driver's details and an estimated arrival time. As the driver moves, the location changes. The ETA changes. The app needs to keep updating.

At the end of the journey, payment happens. A receipt may be generated. A rating may be requested. All of this has to work together as one experience.

And behind that experience there may be mobile applications, APIs, backend services, microservices, databases, events, cloud infrastructure, security, monitoring, maps, payment systems — and increasingly AI and machine learning.

Customers don't experience our architecture.
They experience the product.

That's why I want you to become curious about systems, not only screens.

When you order food, what happens after you tap Order? When you make a payment, where does that request travel? When Netflix recommends something, what happened behind that recommendation? When an OTP arrives in two seconds, what happened during those two seconds?

Once you start asking those questions, technology becomes much more interesting. You stop seeing only an app. You start seeing a system.

04 — Road Map · Speaker Reference

Okay... what exactly
should I learn?

Now this is usually where students look at this huge technology world and think: “Fine... but where do I start?”

There is AI, cloud, cybersecurity, data, APIs, DevOps, automation, containers, programming languages — and a new tool seems to appear every week.

My suggestion is: don't try to learn everything at once. Build your knowledge in layers.

Layer 1 — Learn to Think

Start with problem solving. Learn programming fundamentals. Understand data structures and algorithms. More importantly, learn to take one big problem and break it into smaller problems. A language can change. The ability to think through a problem travels with you.

Layer 2 — Learn How Things Connect

Understand what happens between a frontend, an API, a backend service and a database. Follow the data. When you click a button, where does the request go? What comes back? Once you understand connections, real systems become much easier to reason about.

Layer 3 — Learn Quality

Don't only ask, “Does it work?” Ask, “How can it fail?” What if the value is negative? What if the user clicks twice? What if the network fails? What if a dependency is unavailable? What if a million users arrive? Learn to question what you build and ask, “Can I trust it?”

Layer 4 — Learn How Software Reaches the Real World

Learn Git. Understand CI/CD. Understand automated tests, containers, cloud and deployment. Don't stop at “my code works on my laptop.” Learn what happens after you push the code and how it reaches a real user.

Layer 5 — Put AI Across All of It

Use AI while learning, coding, testing, debugging, analysing and exploring. But let AI sit on top of your knowledge and amplify it. Don't use it as a replacement for understanding.

And there is one more skill I don't want you to underestimate: communication.

You may understand something technically, but eventually you will have to explain it to a teammate, a manager, a customer, an interviewer, or perhaps an audience like this one.

Understanding technology is powerful.
Making someone else understand it is another level of skill.

You do not have to become an expert in everything. Nobody is. The tools I used earlier in my career are not exactly the tools I use today, and the tools we use today may not be the tools you use five years from now.

So don't build your entire career around memorising one tool. Learn how to learn. Learn how to ask good questions. Learn how to adapt.
