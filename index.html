02 — Demo · Speaker Reference

Let's build something
with AI

For the next few minutes, imagine I'm simply someone with an idea. I want to build a very simple banking application.

I want a login page, a banking dashboard, an account with a balance, a beneficiary, and a way to transfer money.

Normally, I would think about the application structure. I would create the files, write the code, connect the components, run it, debug it and gradually turn the idea into an application.

But today, I'm going to do something different. I'm simply going to describe my idea to AI.

Now let's see how the application behaves. The account has $1,000; I'll enter $1,500 as the transfer amount.

PERFORM THE TRANSFER

And look at that — the application allows the transfer.

Now we have a problem. The screen looks professional. The application runs. AI produced it very quickly. But one important business rule is missing.

This is exactly the point I want to make. Fast does not automatically mean accurate.

AI can generate incredibly quickly, but that doesn't mean its answer is correct. Maybe our instruction wasn't clear enough. Maybe it made an assumption. Maybe we forgot to mention an edge case. So don't immediately think, “AI said it, so it must be right.”

When AI generates code, ask yourself: “Do I understand what this code is doing?” When it gives you an analysis, ask: “Do these numbers actually make sense?” And when it gives you an idea, ask: “Can I make this idea better?”

AI can give us an answer quickly. Our value is in knowing what question to ask next. Here, that question is: “Should a bank allow me to transfer $1,500 when I only have $1,000?” We can take that finding back, improve the instruction, fix the missing rule, and repeat the cycle.

The lesson is: use AI for speed, but bring your own understanding.

We need to know what we asked for. We need to observe the result. We need to question it. We need to recognise what is missing. And then we can give AI a better instruction and improve it.

Idea→Generate→Observe→Question→Refine

One key takeaway I want to leave you with from this demo is this: when AI gives you an answer, don't stop at “I got an answer.” Go one step further and ask: “Is that answer accurate? Is it what I expected?”

AI can help us move incredibly fast. But we still have to decide where we're going.

03 — Real World Engineering · Speaker Reference

What happens
behind one click?

I also want to touch on how real systems connect to other systems: APIs, databases, cloud platforms, security, notifications and data. We often learn each technology separately. Now let's see how they come together in one experience.

Think of something many of us have used: a ride booking app. Imagine a student named Diya has just finished attending this conference. She walks out of the venue, opens Uber, enters her destination, sees a price and presses Book. That's it. From her point of view, she opened an app, entered a destination and pressed a button.

But let's freeze that moment. The second she presses Book, something much more interesting starts happening behind the screen.

First, the app needs to know where she is. Her phone provides location information through GPS. Then the application needs to find drivers nearby. There could be hundreds of drivers moving around the city. Does it simply choose the one who is physically closest? Not necessarily.

One driver may be only 500 metres away but travelling in the opposite direction. Another may be two kilometres away but able to reach her faster. There may be traffic, and a driver may still be finishing another trip. What looked like a simple Book button has become a decision making problem. The system needs to consider location, availability, direction, routes and traffic.

Somewhere behind that screen, the mobile app talks to APIs. APIs connect to backend services. Services work with databases. Location data keeps changing; maps and traffic information may be involved. Events move between parts of the system, cloud infrastructure keeps services running, security protects data, and algorithms — sometimes including AI and machine learning — can help with decisions and predictions.

📱 App→📍 GPS→API→Services→🚗 Driver→Maps→Payment

Eventually, one driver receives a notification: Diya wants a ride. The driver accepts. Now Diya can see the car and an estimated arrival time: three minutes away. A moment later, she checks again and it still says three minutes. We've all watched that little car move around the map! Behind it, location updates, ETA calculations, route information and notifications keep flowing.

Then the journey starts. Traffic changes, the fare is calculated, the journey finishes, payment is processed, the driver gets paid, Diya receives a receipt, and the app asks her to rate the driver. All of that began with one action: Book a ride.

Now imagine that one service fails when Diya presses Book and nothing happens. She isn't going to say, “There is increased latency between two microservices.” She'll say, “Uber isn't working. Let's move to another app.”

Customers don't experience our architecture.
They experience the product.

That's what I want you to notice. We learn Java, Python, APIs, databases, cloud, AI, DevOps and cybersecurity as separate subjects. In the real world, technology doesn't work in separate boxes. These parts come together to create one experience.

From now on, stay curious about what happens behind the screen. When you order food, what happens after you tap Order? When you make a payment, where does the request go? When Netflix recommends something, what happened behind that recommendation? When an OTP arrives two seconds after you request it, what happened in those two seconds?

You don't need every answer today. Start asking the questions. That curiosity takes you from learning individual technologies to thinking like an engineer.

And that leads to another question: with hundreds of tools and subjects around us — AI, cloud, cybersecurity, data and APIs — what should you actually learn? Let's build a simple roadmap.

04 — Road Map · Speaker Reference

Okay... what exactly
should I learn?

So what should I actually learn? Technology can feel overwhelming today. Every few months another tool appears on LinkedIn, almost telling us, “Learn me immediately!” Please don't panic. You don't have to learn everything.

If I were starting my technology career today, I would build my knowledge in layers.

Layer 1 — Learn to Think

The first layer isn't AI or cloud, or even a particular programming language. Learn to think through a problem. Learn programming fundamentals: conditions, loops, functions, objects and data. Learn basic data structures and algorithms. Practise taking one big problem and breaking it into smaller ones.

Use AI — absolutely use it. But when it gives you code, ask, “Why does this work?” Change something. Break something. Ask it to explain the part you don't understand. That's how the answer becomes your understanding.

Layer 2 — Learn How Things Connect

Once you understand the fundamentals, look beyond one piece of code. Remember our ride booking example? The mobile screen was only one small part of the system. What is an API? What happens between the frontend and backend? Where does the data go? Why do we need a database? How does one service communicate with another? What happens when that communication fails?

You don't need to become an expert in everything. Start following the journey of the data. Working with large enterprise systems has taught me this repeatedly: sometimes your code works perfectly, but the service it calls is down or the database contains unexpected data. Real engineering gets interesting when you understand those connections.

Layer 3 — Learn Quality

This one is close to my heart because I work in quality engineering. I'm not saying everyone must become a tester. I'm saying that whatever you build, learn to question it. Ask both “Does it work?” and “How can it fail?”

Imagine you build a payment app. What if someone enters a negative amount? What if they press Pay twice? What if the network disappears halfway through? What if ten users suddenly become ten thousand? Get into the habit of asking, “Can I trust this?” Our bank demo is a small example of why that question matters.

Layer 4 — Learn How Software Reaches the Real World

You write code today. How does it get from your laptop to thousands or millions of people? Start with the concepts behind Git, automated builds and tests, CI/CD, containers, cloud and deployment. You don't need to become an expert overnight.

Ask what happens after “I push my code.” How is it built? How is it tested automatically? How is it deployed? Where does it run? Once you understand that journey, CI/CD stops being just another abbreviation and starts to make sense.

Layer 5 — Put AI Across All of It

And finally, AI. Use it while learning, coding, testing, debugging and exploring. Let it sit on top of your knowledge and amplify what you can do. You are still responsible for understanding and validating the result.

Tools will change. Models will change. If your fundamentals are strong, AI becomes an amplifier of your ability.

And one final, important layer: communication. You might write brilliant code or discover something nobody else has noticed. Eventually you need to explain it to a teammate, a manager, a customer or an interviewer.

Understanding technology is powerful. Being able to make someone else understand it is another level of skill. Practise explaining what you built, why you built it, what went wrong and what you learned.

Learn to think. Follow the connections.
Question the result. Explain what you learned.

You don't have to master every new tool that appears. Build these layers, stay curious and keep learning. That's a roadmap you can carry with you even when the tools change.
